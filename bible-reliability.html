Can We Trust the Bible?

Has the Bible been changed? Was it only written by men? Explore the evidence from prophecy, archaeology, ancient manuscripts and people who were not Christians.

Many people have heard that the Bible is too old to be trusted, that its words were altered over time, or that religious leaders simply made it all up. Yet the Bible has left behind evidence that can still be examined: ancient copies of its writings, inscriptions naming biblical people, records from other nations, and prophecies whose fulfilment can be compared with history.

These different witnesses do not all answer the same question. A manuscript helps us check what was written. An inscription can confirm a person, place or event. A non-Christian historian can confirm what was known about Jesus and His followers. Prophecy raises a further question: could the Bible’s far-reaching message have come from human foresight alone? Taken together, they give substantial reasons to take Scripture seriously.

1. Written by people — but was the message merely human?

Yes, human beings physically wrote the Bible. Scripture never hides that fact. It names prophets, kings, shepherds, fishermen and apostles who wrote in their own languages and circumstances. But it also makes a much greater claim: “All scripture is given by inspiration of God” (2 Timothy 3:16), and the prophets spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit (2 Peter 1:21). In other words, the human writers were the messengers; God was the source of their message.

The Bible contains 66 books produced across many generations, by writers living in different places and social conditions. Yet one great story runs through them: God created a good world, sin damaged it, God promised a Deliverer, Jesus came to save, and God will finally restore what sin has ruined. Genesis introduces what Revelation brings to its conclusion. This long-range unity is particularly striking when we consider that most of these writers never met one another.

The Bible also tells the truth about its own heroes. It records David’s sin, Solomon’s failures, Peter’s denial and the disciples’ weaknesses. These are hardly flattering portraits designed to glorify their authors. The writings consistently direct attention beyond human ability to the character, promises and power of God. The claim is not that people had no part in writing; it is that people alone do not adequately explain the message.

2. Prophecy: history described before it happens

One of the Bible’s strongest claims is that God knows the future. In Isaiah 46:9–10 He declares that He makes known “the end from the beginning.” Bible prophecy should therefore be examined alongside history, not treated merely as beautiful religious language. A particularly important example is Daniel chapter 2.

King Nebuchadnezzar saw an image with a head of gold, a chest of silver, a middle of brass, legs of iron, and feet of iron mixed with clay. Daniel identified the head as Babylon and described successive kingdoms that would rise after it (Daniel 2:31–45). In the historicist understanding of prophecy, these powers are Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome. The divided feet point to the division of Rome rather than another lasting world empire. The final stone represents God’s future kingdom, which has not yet replaced the kingdoms of this world.

Daniel is not a prophecy invented by a modern church. Fragments of the book were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, showing that it circulated among Jewish readers before the time of Jesus. Those manuscripts do not by themselves settle every debate about when Daniel was originally composed, but they do establish that its message is ancient and was being preserved long before medieval Christianity.

3. Babylon, Tyre and other nations: prophecies with real locations

Isaiah 13:19–22 foretold the downfall and desolation of Babylon, once the glory of kingdoms. Babylon fell to the Medo-Persians in 539 BC and remained inhabited for a time; its decline into ruins took place over a long period. The prophecy is not a claim that the entire city became empty on the night it was conquered. The enduring ruins of the former imperial capital show the astonishing reversal of its once-great power.

Ezekiel 26:3–14 foretold severe judgment against ancient Tyre, including the overthrow of walls and the use of its stones and timber in the sea. Nebuchadnezzar attacked its mainland settlement; later Alexander the Great advanced against the island stronghold by building a causeway. The city known as Tyre still exists today, so the history should not be overstated as if no one ever lived in the region again. The specific ancient city, siege and construction details are what make this prophecy worth investigating.

Ezekiel 29 describes the humbling of Egypt, once one of the great powers of the ancient world. The scattering and continued identity of the Jewish people are also themes of Deuteronomy 28. These examples are reminders that the Bible’s prophecies concern real peoples and empires. The strongest approach is to examine the actual wording of each prophecy and the sequence of events, rather than relying on exaggerated claims.

4. Jesus and the promises made centuries before His birth

Long before Jesus appeared, the Old Testament held out the hope of a coming Messiah. Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem; Isaiah 53 describes a suffering Servant; Psalm 22:18 speaks of garments being divided; and Zechariah 11:12–13 mentions thirty pieces of silver. The Gospel writers present the life, suffering and death of Jesus in connection with these earlier Scriptures (; 26:14–16; 27:35; ).

The importance of these passages is their existence before the Christian era. The Old Testament was already sacred Scripture among the Jewish people, not a book that Christians could later invent from nothing. The Gospels are Christian witnesses to the fulfilments; outside historians provide a separate line of evidence for Jesus’ existence and execution. Keeping those witnesses distinct makes the overall case stronger.

Jesus also foretold the destruction of Jerusalem’s temple (; ). Jerusalem and its temple were destroyed by Roman armies in AD 70. These prophecies invite readers to consider whether Scripture reveals knowledge and purposes beyond human understanding.

5. Archaeology: names and places rise from the ground

Archaeology does not have to prove every sentence of Scripture to be useful. It can independently test the world in which the Bible says events happened. For instance, Sargon, king of Assyria, appears in Isaiah 20:1; Assyrian records and inscriptions preserve his name and campaigns. A ruler once known to many readers mainly through Scripture also speaks from the records of his own kingdom.

The Moabite Stone, or Mesha Stele, records the Moabite king Mesha and his struggle against Israel. It provides an account from the opposing nation and belongs beside 2 Kings 3. It does not repeat the biblical account word for word, but the overlapping rulers and conflict help establish a shared historical setting. The Merneptah Stele, an Egyptian monument from about 1200 BC, contains an early reference to the people of Israel.

The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III shows tribute associated with Jehu of Israel. Babylonian evidence has also illuminated the position of Belshazzar, named in Daniel 5, where older readers once thought the biblical record mistaken. And an inscription found at Caesarea contains the name of Pontius Pilate, the Roman official under whom Jesus was crucified.

Each find confirms something specific: a name, title, people, conflict or government official. None can photograph a miracle or establish the truth of every belief. Yet repeated agreement between ancient records and the Bible is a serious reason not to dismiss its history simply because it is religious.

6. What did people who were not Christians say about Jesus?

The existence of Jesus does not rest only on Christian books. Josephus, a Jewish historian writing in the first century, mentions James as the brother of “Jesus who was called Christ” (Antiquities 20.200). Another longer passage about Jesus in Josephus has a disputed wording, so the shorter reference to James is the clearer example to begin with.

Tacitus, a Roman historian who was not a Christian, wrote about the persecution of Christians under Nero. He identified Christus as the source of their name and reported that He suffered execution during the reign of Tiberius under Pontius Pilate (Annals 15.44). This is valuable testimony from a writer outside the Christian movement that Jesus was connected with a real Roman governor and a real historical execution.

Pliny the Younger, a Roman official, wrote to Emperor Trajan about Christians in his province in the early second century. He described their custom of gathering and singing to Christ as to a god (Letters 10.96). Suetonius also mentions Christians being punished under Nero (Nero 16). These were not Christian preachers trying to build faith. Their reports show that Jesus and His followers were part of the public history of the Roman world.

These writers do not independently prove the resurrection or every event described in the Gospels. What they do confirm is important: Jesus was not simply a character invented centuries later, His execution was known outside the church, and people were worshipping Him very early. That is a much firmer historical starting point than the claim that Christianity was created out of nothing long afterward.

7. Has the Old Testament been changed over the centuries?

Before the printing press, Scripture had to be copied by hand. That is precisely why ancient surviving copies matter. In 1947, the discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls brought to light Jewish biblical manuscripts from long before the oldest complete medieval Hebrew Bible. They include a remarkably complete scroll of Isaiah dating to before Christ.

When the ancient Isaiah scroll was compared with Hebrew copies from roughly a thousand years later, the agreement was striking. There are differences in spelling and some wording, but the text had not become a different book with an entirely new message. These discoveries allow us to test the claim of wholesale rewriting against actual documents separated by many centuries.

There is also no simple story in which one religious group could secretly replace every copy. Hebrew Scripture was treasured and transmitted by Jewish communities; other traditions, including the Samaritan Pentateuch and ancient translations, preserve additional witnesses. Different groups sometimes disagreed sharply with one another, yet their texts can still be compared. This wide preservation is a strong barrier to the idea of a single successful rewrite of the Bible.

8. How was the New Testament preserved?

The original sheets written by the apostles have not survived. But the New Testament was copied, shared, translated and quoted as Christianity spread. Today its text can be studied through early papyrus fragments, later Greek manuscripts, ancient versions in other languages, and quotations in early Christian writings. These witnesses come from different centuries and regions, rather than from one modern organisation.

Fourth-century manuscripts such as Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus are among the important surviving witnesses. The Byzantine manuscript tradition was also widely copied, and it played a major role in the development of the Received Text, used for the New Testament of the King James Version. The existence of these different streams of manuscripts does not mean that the Bible disappeared. It means that scholars can compare its long history of transmission.

More copies make copying differences easier to spot, not harder to hide. Some scribes skipped words; others repeated phrases or made spelling changes. A smaller number of differences affect longer passages, such as Mark 16:9–20 or :11. These differences deserve honest examination. Yet the many surviving witnesses also allow us to identify them openly. The central witness of the New Testament to Jesus, His death and resurrection, and the call to salvation does not depend on one isolated disputed verse.

9. Why are there different Bible versions?

A new translation is not the same thing as a newly invented Bible. Translators must choose how to express ancient Hebrew and Greek in another language. Some translations stay close to the wording of the original; others express what the translators think the passage means. For close study, a careful, more literal translation helps readers see the words before drawing conclusions from them.

There is also a question of which handwritten copies should guide the translation. The King James Version draws its New Testament text principally from the traditional Received Text, connected with the wider Byzantine transmission. Many modern editions use a different critical Greek text and therefore place some words in footnotes or print certain passages differently. The Faithful Witness makes a strong case for the traditional text and recommends the King James Version, with the New King James Version as a helpful choice for readers who find older English difficult.

No English translation is perfect, and disagreement about a particular reading should not be mistaken for proof that the entire Bible was rewritten. Compare passages carefully, notice any footnotes, and study the context. The larger manuscript record gives readers ways to check disputed wording rather than simply accepting a claim that someone secretly changed the whole Bible.

10. Who decided which books belonged in the Bible?

The early church did not begin with a blank sheet and manufacture stories about Jesus centuries later. The apostles and their companions wrote to real Christian communities, and those writings were copied and read in worship. From an early period, Christians also had to distinguish the genuine apostolic writings from later books appearing under impressive names.

The early churches paid attention to whether a writing came from an apostle or someone close to the apostles, whether its teaching was consistent with Scripture and apostolic instruction, and whether it was widely recognised among the congregations. From this perspective, the church did not give inspired books their authority; it recognised writings that already carried that authority. The process took time, but it was not evidence that the New Testament had been invented by a late council.

11. Why these witnesses belong together

History, archaeology, manuscripts and prophecy make different contributions. The Moabite Stone cannot prove salvation; it can test a historical setting. Tacitus cannot establish every Gospel miracle; he can confirm that Jesus’ execution was known outside Christianity. A Dead Sea Scroll cannot tell you whether God exists; it can show what Jewish readers had in their Scriptures long before our modern Bibles. And prophecy asks a question that material remains alone cannot answer: who could know the end from the beginning?

These witnesses reinforce one another. The Scriptures were not created yesterday. Their text has been preserved through copying and comparison. Their historical world is repeatedly illuminated by independent finds. Their central promise of the coming Messiah reaches across centuries, and their message remains united around Jesus Christ. The evidence gives powerful grounds for confidence that the Bible is far more than the product of clever human planning.

The invitation is simple: read the Bible itself. Begin with Daniel 2, Isaiah 53, and John 20. Compare what each passage claims with the evidence presented here. Do not place blind confidence in a slogan, a church leader or an internet accusation. Examine the witnesses and allow Scripture to speak. “The word of our God shall stand for ever” (Isaiah 40:8).

Common questions

Was the Bible written by men?

Yes. God used real human writers, with their own languages and experiences. Its claim is that the Holy Spirit inspired the message they communicated (2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:21). Human handwriting and divine authorship are not opposites.

Could someone have rewritten the Bible hundreds of years later?

Someone could change an individual copy, but that is very different from changing all the copies already spread across different lands, languages and communities. Ancient manuscripts and translations make the history of the text open to comparison.

Do copying differences mean the Bible is unreliable?

No. Differences are real and should not be hidden. Most are small, while some passages need careful study. Because so many witnesses survive, those differences can be identified and weighed; the central message has not vanished.

Why does one version have words that another puts in a footnote?

Translators sometimes work from different manuscript readings, and some choose a closer word-for-word style than others. The KJV and NKJV follow the traditional textual line valued in The Faithful Witness; footnotes in other editions reflect textual decisions, not proof that an entire Bible has been secretly replaced.

How do we know the prophecies were not all written after the events?

Ancient Jewish manuscripts, including pre-Christian copies among the Dead Sea Scrolls, show that many prophetic books existed long before later Christian history. Questions about the date of each prophecy still deserve study. Daniel’s sequence of kingdoms and the Bible’s prophecies about Christ should be examined in their historical order.

What can archaeology actually prove?

It can identify historical people, places, events and cultural details. Finds such as the Mesha Stele, Assyrian records and the Pilate inscription support specific historical points. Archaeology alone does not prove a miracle or every statement of faith.

Did non-Christians really write about Jesus?

Yes. Josephus mentions Jesus in connection with James, Tacitus reports His execution under Pontius Pilate, and Pliny describes early Christians honouring Christ. These are valuable outside witnesses, though they are not eyewitness accounts of every Gospel event.

What makes the Bible different from another ancient religious book?

It invites examination across several lines of evidence at once: preservation of its writings, historical detail, outside witnesses, a unified message across generations, and prophecy. Christians understand this combined witness as pointing to God’s inspiration rather than merely human invention.
