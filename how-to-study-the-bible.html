Introductory Bible Study Guide

How Do You Study the Bible?

A practical (10 Point) guide to discovering what God is saying through His Word

Have you ever opened your Bible, read a passage, and wondered, “What does this really mean?” You are not alone. The Bible contains wonderful truths, but understanding them takes more than simply turning the pages. We need to approach God’s Word with the right attitude and learn how to study it carefully.

Imagine receiving a detailed map to a place you have always wanted to visit. You could admire the map, keep it in a drawer, or talk about how useful it is. But it will not help you reach your destination until you open it, follow its directions, and keep checking that you are on the right road. The Bible is much the same. It is meant to guide our lives, not merely sit on a shelf. Psalm 119:72 reminds us that God’s instruction is worth more than riches (see also Proverbs 8:10–11).

You do not need a theology degree or a shelf full of expensive books to begin. You need a Bible, time to read it, a willingness to learn, and a desire to know God. Here are some principles that can help you make the most of your study.

1. Begin by Asking God for Understanding

Before you begin reading, talk to God. Ask Him to help you understand what you read and to show you how it applies to your life. It does not need to be a long prayer. What matters is that you sincerely want His guidance.

Why is prayer important? Because the Bible is not simply another book of human ideas. God inspired its message, and we need His Spirit to help us understand spiritual things. Jesus described His words as spirit and life (John 6:63), while 1 Corinthians 2:14 explains why spiritual truth cannot be properly understood through human thinking alone.

James 1:5 gives us a wonderful promise: if we lack wisdom, we can ask God, who gives generously. Think of trying to assemble something with unfamiliar instructions. Would it not help to speak with the person who designed it? When we study Scripture, we can ask its divine Author for help.

Prayer also reminds us to listen rather than trying to make the Bible agree with what we already believe. A simple prayer might be: “Lord, help me understand Your Word and give me a willing heart to follow it.”

2. Read It Again—and Keep Reading

Do not be discouraged if you do not understand a passage the first time. Some things become clear only when we read them again, think about them, and compare them with other parts of the Bible. Even experienced students of Scripture continue to discover things they had not noticed before.

Think about learning a new route through an unfamiliar city. On the first trip, you are concentrating on finding the right turns. After travelling the same route several times, you begin to notice landmarks and connections you missed before. Repeated Bible reading works in a similar way. Familiar passages can take on greater meaning as we see how they fit together.

Make time for regular study and prayer. It is better to build a steady habit than to wait until you happen to feel like reading. You can begin with a manageable amount and grow from there. The important thing is to keep returning to God’s Word with an open mind.

3. Be Willing to Follow What You Discover

Studying the Bible is not just about collecting information. God wants His Word to make a difference in the way we think, speak, and live. Jesus said that those who are willing to do God’s will can come to know whether His teaching is from God (John 7:17). James 1:22 likewise urges us to be doers of the Word, not hearers only.

Suppose you visit a doctor because you want to improve your health. The doctor explains what needs to change, but you decide to ignore the advice and keep doing exactly what you did before. You may have gained information, but you have not allowed it to help you. Bible study is like that when we learn what God says but refuse to act on it.

Sometimes the truth challenges something we enjoy or an opinion we have held for years. That is when our willingness is tested. Jesus told His disciples that there were things they were not yet ready to receive (John 16:12). We should not assume that understanding everything at once is the goal. Instead, let us be faithful to the light we already have and ask God to help us become willing where we struggle.

4. Let the Bible Say What It Says

One of the most important habits in Bible study is to allow Scripture to speak for itself. Rather than starting with a conclusion and hunting for a verse to support it, begin by asking, “What is this passage actually saying?” Second Timothy 2:15 encourages us to handle the Word of truth carefully.

Imagine a detective investigating an incident. If the detective decides who is guilty before looking at the evidence, every new detail may be forced to fit that assumption. A careful investigator does the opposite: examines the evidence first and then reaches a conclusion. We should approach the Bible with that same care.

Read the words in their natural sense unless the passage gives good reason to understand them symbolically. The Bible certainly uses prophecy, symbols, poetry, and illustrations, but not every ordinary statement has a hidden meaning. Be prepared to change your understanding when Scripture shows you something different from what you expected.

5. Read the Whole Passage, Not Just One Verse

A verse can be misunderstood when it is separated from the verses around it. Ask who is speaking, who is being addressed, what question is being answered, and what happens before and after the statement. Sometimes a few surrounding verses are enough. At other times you need the whole chapter—or even the whole book.

Consider receiving a message that says, “I never said he took the money.” Without knowing the conversation, you might misunderstand what the writer meant or was denying. Context changes how words are understood. It matters just as much when reading Scripture.

A useful habit is to read the paragraph, then the chapter, and then look at how the passage fits into the larger message of the book. Do not build an important belief on a sentence you have not understood in its setting.

6. Understand the Time, Place, Culture, and Language

The Bible was written in real places, to real people, in cultures that were often very different from our own. Knowing something about their circumstances can make a difficult passage much clearer. Ask what customs, geography, or historical events may be important to the meaning.

For example, when Jesus discussed the practice called “Corban” (Mark 7:9–13), He was addressing a religious tradition that could be used to avoid caring for one’s parents. Knowing the custom helps us see why Jesus condemned it: a human tradition was being used to set aside God’s commandment.

Language matters too. The Bible was originally written in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek, and translations sometimes cannot capture every shade of meaning in a single English word. You do not have to learn those languages to understand the Bible, but checking a reliable word study can sometimes help. For instance, the Greek word translated “bottomless pit” in Revelation 20:1 is often discussed in connection with the word abyss. Its meaning should be examined in the context of the passage and related texts, rather than by imagining a literal hole running through the earth.

Historical background and word meanings are useful aids, but they should help us understand the passage—not become excuses to read our own ideas into it.

7. Use Study Tools, but Keep Scripture First

Bible dictionaries, maps, concordances, cross-references, and commentaries can all be valuable, and many of these resources are now available right on our phones. A map can show where events happened. A concordance can help you find related passages. A dictionary can explain an unfamiliar term. A commentary can introduce you to another student’s understanding of a difficult text.

Think of a builder working with a good set of tools. A measuring tape, level, and square make the job easier and help reveal mistakes. But the tools do not replace the building plan. In the same way, study aids can support our reading, but the Bible remains the authority against which explanations must be tested.

Remember that a commentary is someone’s interpretation. Even a respected writer can be mistaken. Use helpful resources gratefully, but do not treat them as inspired Scripture. And if you have only a Bible to begin with, begin anyway.

8. Compare Scripture with Scripture Before Reaching a Conclusion

A single verse may not tell us everything the Bible teaches about a subject. When studying an important question, gather the relevant passages and examine them together. Second Corinthians 13:1 speaks of establishing a matter by two or three witnesses, and Isaiah 28:10 describes instruction coming piece by piece. These passages remind us of the value of careful, patient examination rather than hasty conclusions.

Think about a court case. Would you want a verdict based on one short statement, with the rest of the evidence ignored? Of course not. A fair decision considers all the relevant testimony. In Bible study, we should also resist forming a doctrine from one difficult or isolated verse while overlooking clearer passages on the same subject.

For example, a passage in 1 Corinthians 11 about head coverings raises questions about worship and the customs of that time. Before turning one detail into a universal rule, examine the full discussion, its historical setting, and other relevant biblical teaching. Let the combined evidence guide your conclusion.

When passages seem to disagree, do not immediately assume one must be wrong. Read them again in context and ask whether they are addressing different situations or different aspects of the same truth. The goal is not to win an argument but to understand what God has revealed.

9. Be Careful with Personal Interpretations

It is easy to say, “I feel this verse means…” or “I have a special understanding no one else has.” Feelings and personal impressions may prompt us to ask questions, but they are not proof that an interpretation is correct. Second Peter 1:20–21 teaches that prophecy did not originate in human will; men spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit.

Proverbs 11:14 points to the value of wise counsel. Studying with other thoughtful believers can expose weaknesses in our reasoning and help us notice passages we have overlooked. This does not mean that the majority is always right, or that we should accept an interpretation simply because a teacher is confident.

The believers in Berea give us a helpful example. They listened carefully to Paul, but they also searched the Scriptures to check whether his teaching was true (Acts 17:10–11). That is a healthy balance: be willing to learn from others, yet test what you hear against the Bible itself. Acts 20:30 also warns that misleading teachings can arise even from within the community of believers.

Be especially cautious when someone claims to have a unique revelation, dismisses reasonable questions, or builds an entire teaching on a few unclear verses. God’s Word deserves more careful treatment than that.

10. Put These Principles into Practice

Here is a simple way to approach your next study. Start with prayer. Read the passage slowly, then read it again. Identify the main message and look at the surrounding verses. Notice anything you do not understand. Check the historical setting or a word meaning when it is relevant. Find other passages that speak about the same subject. Consider helpful study resources, but compare every explanation with Scripture. Finally, ask what the passage is calling you to believe or do.

You may not answer every question in one sitting. That is all right. Write down what you discover and return to the difficult parts later. Bible study is not a race, and there is no prize for reaching a conclusion before you have examined the evidence.

A Final Encouragement

The purpose of Bible study is not merely to know more about the Bible. It is to get to know Jesus better, understand His will, and allow His Word to shape our lives. Each time we open Scripture, we have an opportunity to listen, learn, and grow.

So why not begin today? Open your Bible, ask God for wisdom, and take the time to discover what He is saying. Read carefully, ask questions, compare the evidence, and be willing to follow what you learn. The more faithfully you study, the more you will begin to see how the different parts of God's Word fit together.

All you need is an open Bible, a willing heart, and a desire to learn.
